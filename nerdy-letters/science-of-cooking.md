## How to boil water

Some of us that can't cook joke that: "I can boil water." Now let me say something mildly controversial. HOW CAN YOU CLAIM YOU CAN DO SOMETHING YOU DON'T UNDERSTAND?? 

Hey hey, before you drag my baby locs, hear me out. We use many words we don't understand, but can perceive. In fact, the term "boiling" likely starts from our perception of how fluids behave when put under intense heat. Essentially, the application of energy into fluid. I said I will write this without being very precise with the science, and focus on portraying a picture in our minds. 

The boiling point of a fluid is the temperature at which the fluid begins to transition from the liquid phase to the gas phase. A congruent train of thought exists for melting things as well, think melting a block of chocolate: phase transition from solid to liquid. So we know if we put a pot of water on a stove long enough, the fluid particles will become hot enough to boil, at this point they become energetic and transition to a gas phase, which we call "steam." The reason why cooking is a science and an art is because we do not need to precisely describe these concepts. A plate of dish marries this latent knowledge of science with the creativity to exploit the perception without the full description (which is the science). 

Now you might be thinking: "why the hell is boiling water so important?" Okay, let's talk about it, and connect it to the science of cooking Nigeria's most popular dish export--Jollof Rice. 

## Cooking Jollof Rice
Some of us have seen popular YouTube videos and TikToks, giving people tips for how to cook jollof quickly and conveniently. Honestly, I think it is cray these days how so much content is to "optimize." Reduce the time spent cooking jollof. Reduce your time eating. Code faster. Build faster. Bro, tell us how to reduce our time spent on these apps!! 

Anyway, back to jollof. For those that don't know, Jollof is a popular Nigerian (West African) dish that uses tomatoes, red bell peppers, scotch bonnets, onions, garlic, ginger, to create a savory and aromatic rice dish. 

These days people suggest to oven roast or bake your tomato, onions, and peppers prior to blending for cooking. Some say it is because it cooks faster and reduces your time spent checking the pot. This is true, but why?

Remember we talked about boiling? Now, let's talk about water. Another thing I failed to mention is what happens when water is boiling. At the boiling point of water, every additional heat that is going into the pot is not increasing the temperature of the water but rather your water stays fixed at 100C while the steam or vapor escapes. This is called latent heat. I think we named it latent because it is a form of energy in the water particles that cannot be perceived as a temperature change. Beyond the boiling, it won't get hotter (it can, but for our cooking, it won't). But hey, we know the hotter things are, the faster they cook! 

The idea of roasting our peppers is to remove moisture (a.k.a water). When we cook jollof rice, a huge percentage of our time is spent boiling water, and many of us don't realize this. Tomatoes, bell peppers, and onions jointly are around 90% water, thus a decent amount of time is spent boiling that water to release the moisture, so the flesh can cook. Additionally, the reason boiling is also important is that it tames the acids in tomatoes--these acids give the natural sour taste we want to get rid of. The acids don't boil off, but as the water leaves, the sugars concentrate and caramelize, and that sweetness balances out the sourness (compare this to a red wine reduction sauce, where heating actually does remove the alcohol). So what oven roasting gives us is a passive task (just throw your tomatoes and peppers in the oven while you sleep!) that can gain us time via dehydration. Many of our stoves would have had to do this while cooking, which can waste our time checking the pot every ten minutes. An additional benefit of roasting is that if you get the oven hot enough to char the vegetables and tomatoes, it adds another depth of flavor to the dish (some argue even a hint of smokiness but I can argue that later LOL). So yeah boiling is fundamental to every dish and the understanding of water contents and boiling points can come off as mastery and finesse in the kitchen.

Another interesting and fortunate angle to this is the fact that we can raise the boiling point/temperature of water just by adding salt. This is explained in detail by Raoult's law in thermodynamics; when I learned this in school, Jollof rice came to mind. So essentially if water boils at 100C, if we add salt to it, we raise the boiling temperature of the solution to let's say 100.3C. This is a modest but useful gain that essentially means that now we can get the pot of water hotter than 100C, allowing your rice to absorb moisture before everything evaporates (escapes because it has phase transitioned from liquid to more energetic gas)! And, we know salt also gives our food flavor anyway! This is cool! 

Hmm...how fortunate are we as humans that the world works in our favor? We should all ponder on this. I would argue that most of the best chefs may not know thermodynamics or Raoult's law or the boiling point of alcohol, but their perception and ability to conjure these ideas in the kitchen are what makes it an art as well. To create is always to understand, but that understanding can take many forms and the expression of that understanding is art in its own right.
